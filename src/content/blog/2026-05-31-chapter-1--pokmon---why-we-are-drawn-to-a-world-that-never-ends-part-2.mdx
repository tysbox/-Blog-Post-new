      **III. The World That Never Ends**

      \*Is it possible that we love what we love precisely because it never quite belongs to us?　\*

      \*　\*

      While Western narratives tend to trace a straight line toward a definitive conclusion, Shintō time is cyclical. Just as the seasons turn and festivals repeat, the world does not end. It is rearranged, renewed, re-tended. The world does not arrive. It is mended.

      The structure of the Pokémon series lives this sense of time faithfully. One journey closes and the world—of course—continues. New regions appear with fresh palettes and unfamiliar presences. The same shape of adventure is picked up and carried forward, not as repetition but as renewal.

      This mirrors the concept of tokowaka—perpetual newness. Just as the Ise Grand Shrine is ritually rebuilt every twenty years through the shikinen sengu, so that the shrine stays alive rather than becoming fossilized, the world of Pokémon achieves its kind of eternity through the motion of regeneration itself. There is permanence here, but not a final version.

      Completing the Pokédex or becoming a Champion feels less like a destination and more like a marker along a walk. The world remains, deliberately, unfinished. The premise that we can never fully grasp all of it is left quiet at the bottom of the narrative, like an open well—always there, never fully drawn from.



      **IV. The Unintentional Design**

      *How much of what we find "natural" was never designed at all, but inherited?*

      The people who made Pokémon did not set out with Shintō as a manifesto. The starting point was much simpler—a deeply personal, bodily experience: the childhood impulse to chase beetles through grass, to catch what moves and fits into your palm.

      But here is what I find most interesting. In Japanese culture, Shintō does not arrive as doctrine. There is no creed to convert to, no list of articles to memorize. It functions as what I call a Cultural OS—a set of behavioral defaults loaded beneath everything else, running in the background like an operating system you never chose but always inherit.

      The instinct to treat nature as a partner to relate to, rather than a resource to manage. The habit of resolving trouble not by declaring an enemy but by calming and coexisting. The quiet certainty that a story which simply does not end is not strange at all, but the natural state of things. These elements were woven into the grammar of the work without anyone sitting down to plan it, and they settle into the texture of the thing like grain in a piece of wood—felt before named.

      The result is a world of Pokémon that carries no religious marking on its surface and yet stands, from the inside, as Shintō down to its grain. It is not an ideology. It is a shared sensibility—the particular feel of a world that you recognize the way you recognize your own language spoken in another room.



      **Conclusion: A Modern Myth Lived**

      The myriad spirits, an ethics of restoration, and a cyclical time that refuses to hand us a last word—these three keep the story updating, keep it alive.

      What I find most important, and perhaps most tender, is that this world does not claim to be the only right way or preach from any position of moral authority. As a vessel—open, unclaimed—people from everywhere can enter and layer their own journeys upon each other, quietly, without collision.

      For me, writing this was also an attempt to give a name to the "Shintō OS"—to put words around an order I have always felt inside me but never articulated. The most powerful forces in a culture are often those that operate most quietly, unseen and unexplained—the thing we live by before we learn its name.
